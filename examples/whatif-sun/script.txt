What would happen if the sun suddenly vanished? The first thing you'd notice is nothing. For eight minutes and twenty seconds, absolutely nothing.

Here's why. Sunlight takes about eight minutes and twenty seconds to reach us. And the sun's gravity travels at the same speed. So for those eight minutes the sun is gone, but the sky stays bright, and Earth keeps circling the empty spot where it used to be.

Then the lights go out. Night falls over the whole planet at the same moment, even at noon. And Earth stops curving. Without the sun's pull, it flies off in a straight line, at about eighteen miles every second.

One hour in, the sky looks strange. The Moon is gone. It never made its own light, it only reflected the sun, so it's still there, just invisible. The planets vanish too. Only the stars are left.

By tomorrow, every solar panel on Earth is dead. Photosynthesis has stopped everywhere, in the grass, in the trees, and in the tiny plankton that make about half of the oxygen we breathe. But the air still holds plenty. We wouldn't run out for a very long time.

After a week, the cold takes over. Small ponds freeze solid. Lakes take weeks or months. Most plants are dead within a few weeks, though big trees could hang on for decades, living off stored sugar.

A year later, the surface of the oceans is frozen over. But underneath, the water stays liquid, because the ice acts like a blanket. Scientists say the oceans would not freeze solid for hundreds of thousands of years.

And deep on the seafloor, hot vents keep warming the water. Microbes there don't need sunlight at all. They eat chemicals from the rock. Life would not disappear. It would just move underwater.

Of course, nothing we know of could make the sun vanish. But next time you watch a sunset, remember: if it ever did, you would get eight more minutes of sunshine. Enjoy them.
